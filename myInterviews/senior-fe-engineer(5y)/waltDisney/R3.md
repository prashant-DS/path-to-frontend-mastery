# Discussion Round — Round 3

## Overview

Video call, purely conversational. No coding, no whiteboard, no shared editor — an
hour of open discussion.

The interviewer opened with her side: what she works on, what the team is building,
what she likes about it and what she doesn't. Then she turned it around and asked me
about myself and my frontend journey.

Because it was this loose and friendly, I don't remember the full sequence of the
conversation. What is below is what I actually recall; the rest is a gap.

---

## My Frontend Journey

I walked through it company by company rather than as a list of skills.

### First company — an initiative that did not fly

We were using a third-party vendor for one of our capabilities. I volunteered to build
an in-house replacement and we took it to a POC.

Technically it worked — I got browser-to-browser video calls working end to end. It
still did not ship, for a reason that had nothing to do with the frontend:

- We were in a banking system, which required server-side controls on the calls.
- Implementing those server-side controls meant pulling in _another_ third-party
  library, which brought back the same problems we were trying to escape: maintenance,
  upstream bug fixes, dependency on someone else's release cycle.
- Since we would be leaning on a third party either way, the security-conscious call
  was to stay with the existing vendor rather than take on an in-house surface.

I brought this up deliberately. The point I wanted to make was not that the project
succeeded — it didn't — but that I started it, took it far enough to make the tradeoff
real, and learned from where it stopped.

### Second company — the AI work

Moved on to the actual initiatives at my current company, mostly the AI-adjacent work:
MCP server work, tool creation, and public-facing API work.

I volunteered to lead the MCP onboarding for our team. Context on how that was set up:

- The company already had a central MCP server acting as a wrapper — it aggregates the
  MCP servers defined by each individual team and exposes the combined surface to
  customers.
- That wrapper handles auth, rate limiting, and the rest of the cross-cutting concerns
  before forwarding a request down to a team's MCP server.
- My scope was the complete onboarding of _our_ app into that: the MCP app, the MCP
  layer, the backend APIs behind it — end-to-end ownership of getting our app exposed
  as an MCP server.

Worth noting this was not a top-down company initiative. It came from end users
directly asking for our data as an MCP server so they could use it from their own AI
clients.

---

## Why I Took the MCP Work On

Explained the way I actually think about it: frontend is a medium for connecting a user
to the backend and the database. The web browser is one way of doing that. An MCP
server is another. An API is another.

So leading the MCP work did not feel like leaving frontend — it felt like the same job
through a different surface.

### The part I am not sold on

I also gave her the flip side, which is where I think the industry is heading and why
it bothers me.

The interaction surface may shift from the browser to the AI client. The problem is
that neither we as developers nor the user has much control over that client. The AI
client works with its own brain — it can interpret, drop, or reshape things based on
the model and on context that is already failing.

Concretely: even if we return exactly the right data from the API, we cannot guarantee
the AI client relays that same data back to the user. Which means we give up control of
the user experience the moment we move from the browser to the AI client.

---

## Where I Want to Go

She asked how I see myself in all of this — do I want to stay a frontend engineer who
uses AI, or something else.

My answer was that I had considered switching to backend, on the assumption that
frontend will not stand alone going forward. I ruled that out myself: backend is
another core technology, and I did not want to trade one core for another. I would
rather keep frontend as my core and treat AI as the budding technology layered on top.

Where that lands me is the platform side — building the AI capabilities _of_ a frontend
app, or of the app as a whole. Frontend plus AI, not a developer who merely uses AI
tooling, but one shipping customer-facing AI features.

I was honest that my core forte is still frontend: discussion, optimisation, solving
performance bottlenecks, building things that are scalable and reliable. But I have
been leaning into the AI side and owning pieces of it, both because the industry is
shifting and because it is shifting inside my company. And I said plainly that it is
still an open sea — there is no straightforward path in AI right now on where to go.

---

## UI Design and What AI Is Doing to It

I brought up something I have been unhappy about: with AI in the picture, designers are
being laid off, and product managers are handing the frontend engineer an HTML draft
directly as the design.

I said I don't like that, and gave the reasoning:

- A designer carries the whole company theme, the colour palette, the design system.
  A product manager generally does not hold all of that.
- AI does not close that gap either. It will make a draft _better_, but it does not
  solve design.
- Design is a major lever on user experience, and it is currently the part getting the
  least focus.

### The example I used

At one of my companies we had a call screen with two buttons: one to disconnect the
call, one to rotate the camera. The buttons looked good and the icons were fine.

Consumers still could not use them. When a user on a call was asked to switch their
camera, a meaningful number of them could not work out which button to press.

The point I drew from it: this is not about the UI being objectively good or bad. It is
about the end user's perspective. Good design in the abstract is not the same as good
design _for the people actually using your product_ — and if you know who your users
are, you have to reason from how they will perceive the screen, not from how clean it
looks.

### What we did

We colour-coded the two actions: green for rotate camera, red for disconnect.

That is not the industry-standard way to treat those buttons. But our click-through
rate on them went up noticeably after the change — and that only worked because we knew
our customers well enough to predict how they would read the UI.
